## column-1 [column] Column 1

[c1-l001] Faſu. *Hum peixe do rio.*
[c1-l001 note] Hasu here names a river fish. The following identical-looking headword has a plant sense, so the entries should not be merged merely because their kana coincide.
[c1-l002] Faſu. *Golfão. ¶ Item, Certas bustellas da*
[c1-l002 note] Hasu is glossed by the aquatic plant name golfão, followed by a separate scalp-sore sense. The Portuguese plant comparison need not be treated as a precise modern botanical identification.
[c1-l003 >] *cabeça.*
[c1-l003 note] Completes the description of sores or scabs on the head. It belongs to the second meaning introduced by Item, not the aquatic plant gloss.
[c1-l004] Faſu iqe. *A lagoa com golfãos.*
[c1-l004 note] Hasu ike is a lotus pond: ike identifies the body of water. The Portuguese says a pond containing the aquatic plants just defined.
[c1-l005] Faſune. *Certas bustellas da cabeça.*
[c1-l005 note] Hasune repeats the scalp-sore sense. Despite the tempting modern analysis hasu-ne, lotus root, the printed Portuguese explicitly describes a condition of the head.
[c1-l006] Fata, l, fataqe. *Campo que ſe laura, ou ſe-*
[c1-l006 note] Hata and hatake are alternatives for cultivated upland ground. The following crops help distinguish this from an irrigated rice field.
[c1-l007 >] *mea de trigo, grãos, &c.*
[c1-l007 note] Completes semea: the field is sown with wheat, grains or pulses. The line is the agricultural definition's continuation, not another headword.
[c1-l008] Fata. *Borda de qualquer couſa.*
[c1-l008 note] Hata is an edge or border. The scan's d ascender supports borda, not borra; the latter would mean residue and would conflict with the Japanese sense.
[c1-l009] Fata. *Tear. ¶ Item, liços de tear.*
[c1-l009 note] This hata is a loom and, secondarily, its heddles. Tear is the Portuguese noun for the weaving apparatus, not an English verb.
[c1-l010] Fata. *Bandeira. ¶* Fatauo maqu. *En-*
[c1-l010 note] Hata now means a flag. Hata wo maku is rolling up the flag, confirmed by enrolar across the line break.
[c1-l011 >] *rolar a bandeira. ¶* Fatauo aguru. *Ar*
[c1-l011 note] Hata wo aguru means raising the flag. The scan supports Fatauo rather than Fitauo, and the Portuguese begins arvorar, to hoist.
[c1-l012 >] *uorar a bandeira. ¶* Fatauo ſaſu. *Le-*
[c1-l012 note] Ar-uorar gives arvorar, hoisting a flag; the visible rounded vowel supports uorar, not uirar. Hata wo sasu then describes wearing or carrying the flag fixed at the belt.
[c1-l013 >] *uar a bandeira na cinta. ¶* Fataſaxi. *O*
[c1-l013 note] Completes leuar and specifies the belt. Hatasashi is the person carrying the flag; the following relative clause supplies the definition.
[c1-l014 >] *que leua a bandeira. ¶* Fatazauo. *Cana*
[c1-l014 note] Ends the flag-bearer definition and introduces hatazao, the flagstaff. Zao is the voiced compound form of sao, a pole.
[c1-l015 >] *ou haſtea em que ſe enrola a bandeira.*
[c1-l015 note] The flagstaff is a cane or shaft around which the flag is rolled. This connects with maku in the first flag example.
[c1-l016] Fatabari. *Largura de taboa, peça, &c.*
[c1-l016 note] Hatabari is breadth or width. Portuguese taloa is suspicious: taboa, a board, is plausible in this context, but the damaged middle letter is not resolved confidently enough to substitute b here.
[c1-l017 >] *¶ Item, Poſſe, ou faculdade. ¶* Fata-
[c1-l017 note] Hatabari also means means, resources, or capacity. Posse/faculdade marks this figurative breadth of circumstances, illustrated by the following first-person statement.
[c1-l018 >] barimo nai *m*igia. *Não tenho poſſe, nem fa-*
[c1-l018 note] Completes hatabari mo nai mi ja: a person without means or support. Migia represents mi ja, not an unintelligible single lexical item; the Portuguese says that the speaker lacks resources.
[c1-l019 >] *to, nem parentes, &c.*
[c1-l019 note] Completes fato and adds the absence of relatives. The list concerns possessions and social support, not merely the abstract ability to perform a task.
[c1-l020] Fatacari, u, atta. *Estar em pè com as pernas*
[c1-l020 note] Hatakaru means standing with the legs spread. The conjugational abbreviations belong to a posture verb, not to hatake, the field.
[c1-l021 >] *abertas, & largas.*
[c1-l021 note] Describes the legs as open and wide apart. This completes the posture definition and confirms the spatial sense of hatakaru.
[c1-l022] Fatachi. *Idade de 20. annos.*
[c1-l022 note] Hatachi is the age of twenty. The Arabic numeral and its following point are part of the printed explanation and are preserved.
[c1-l023] Fatafatato. *Adu. Apreſſadamente. ¶* Fa-
[c1-l023 note] Hata-hata to is an adverb of haste or quick activity. The next example negates that quality, describing a slow person rather than repeating the positive definition.
[c1-l024 >] tafatatomo nai fito. *Homem rude, & tardo.*
[c1-l024 note] Together with Fa- this reads hatahata to mo nai hito, someone lacking quickness. The Portuguese rude/tardo describes dullness or slowness, not simply bad manners.
[c1-l025] Fatagaxira. *Ponta das bandeiras. ¶* Fa-
[c1-l025 note] Hatagashira is the top or tip of flags, not necessarily a commander despite the possible human sense of kashira elsewhere. The Portuguese definition makes the physical meaning explicit.
[c1-l026 >] tagaxiraga nabiqu, l, miyuru. *Menearẽ*
[c1-l026 note] Completes hatagashira ga nabiku, or miyuru: flag tips flutter or become visible. The alternative marker separates two predicates with the same subject.
[c1-l027 >] *ſe, ou aparecerem as pontas das bandeiras no*
[c1-l027 note] Completes menearem-se and supplies aparecerem for miyuru. The flag tips are the subjects of both Portuguese verbs.
[c1-l028 >] *arrayal.*
[c1-l028 note] Locates the visible or fluttering flags in the military encampment. Arrayal is the camp in this context, not a festival.
[c1-l029] Fatago. *Comida de eſtalagem. ¶* Fatagouo
[c1-l029 note] Hatago here means an inn meal. This differs from hatagoya, the establishment, defined below; the example treats hatago as the object of eating.
[c1-l030 >] cǔ. *Comer em eſtalagem.*
[c1-l030 note] Completes hatago wo kū, eating at an inn. The scan shows a two-sided caron above u, not a single grave stroke; the long-vowel mark is retained as cǔ.
[c1-l031] Fatagoxen. *O que ſe paga polla comida na eſ-*
[c1-l031 note] Hatagosen is payment for the inn meal. Xen represents sen, money, and the Portuguese explicitly distinguishes the charge from the food itself.
[c1-l032 >] *talagem.*
[c1-l032 note] Completes estalagem, the inn where the meal is paid for. This is the end of the fee definition, before the separate establishment entry.
[c1-l033] Fatagoya. *Estalagem, ou venda.*
[c1-l033 note] Hatagoya is the inn or roadside lodging establishment. Venda is a place-name noun for such an establishment here, not simply the action of selling.
[c1-l034] Fataita. *Parede de taboas pouco aleuantada.*
[c1-l034 note] Hataita denotes a low wall or screen of boards. Ita is board; the Portuguese specifies limited height rather than a full-height partition.
[c1-l035] Fatamata. *Alem diſſo. S.*
[c1-l035 note] Hatamata means furthermore or besides that. The following S. is a register label in the explanatory typography, not a Japanese initial.
[c1-l036] Fatameqi, u, eita. *Andar apreſſado, & ſol*
[c1-l036 note] Hatameku describes hurried, busy activity. The Portuguese adjective continues across the break as sollicito; the double l belongs to the historical spelling.
[c1-l037 >] *licito ſobre algũa couſa.*
[c1-l037 note] The scan shows initial l, completing sollicito after sol above, not solsícito. The phrase describes being actively concerned or busy about something.
[c1-l038] Fatameqimauari, u, atta. *Andar rodeando*
[c1-l038 note] Hatameki-mawaru adds going around to busy or hurried activity. The gloss continues with the manner of that movement below.
[c1-l039 >] *com preſſa, & fadiga.*
[c1-l039 note] Haste and exertion qualify going around. Fadiga need not imply that the person has already stopped from exhaustion.
[c1-l040] Fatamono. *Tear.*
[c1-l040 note] Hatamono is a loom in this entry. Its very short gloss should remain separate from the execution apparatus defined under the same headword immediately afterward.
[c1-l041] Fatamono. *Forca em que crucificão ao modo*
[c1-l041 note] This hatamono is an execution frame used for Japanese-style crucifixion. The frame-like shape may explain the shared designation, but the two senses are explicitly separated in print.
[c1-l042 >] *de Iapão.*
[c1-l042 note] Completes ao modo de Iapão, specifying the Japanese manner. It qualifies crucifixion, not the loom definition two lines earlier.
[c1-l043] Fataqe. *Quintal, orta, campo, onde ſe ſemea*
[c1-l043 note] Hatake is a garden or cultivated field. The next line's explicit exclusion of rice is important to its agricultural sense.
[c1-l044 >] *qualquer couſa tirando arroz.*
[c1-l044 note] Anything except rice may be sown there. Tirando means excepting in this construction and supports the upland-field versus paddy distinction.
[c1-l045] Fataqe, uru, eta. *Vt,* Te *a*xiuo fataqu-
[c1-l045 note] Hatakeru is now a verb, illustrated by spreading hands and feet. The example is printed fataqu- followed by ru; its qu spelling is retained rather than mechanically assimilated to the headword's qe.
[c1-l046 >] ru. *Estender as mãos, & abrir as pernas.*
[c1-l046 note] The scan clearly supplies ru and a point, completing fataquru rather than the old ni reading. Portuguese confirms stretching the hands and spreading the legs; the unusual vowel sequence is preserved as printed.
[c1-l047] Fataqegaſa. *Hũas bustellas brancas.*
[c1-l047 note] Hatakegasa is a skin condition described as white scabs or patches. This is a disease term, not a field or a sunshade despite the possible modern associations of its components.

[c2-l001] Fataracaxe, ſuru, eta. *Fazer trabalhar, pe-*
[c2-l001 note] Hatarakaseru is causative: make someone work or fight. The dictionary's suru abbreviation belongs to the historical conjugational notation and is not treated as a separate modern suru compound.
[c2-l002 >] *lejar, &c.*
[c2-l002 note] Completes pelejar, to fight. This extends the causative beyond ordinary labor to military action.
[c2-l003] Fataracaxi, ſu, aita. *Vſar, ou exercitar.*
[c2-l003 note] Hatarakasu means putting something to use or exercising it. The following examples apply it to intelligence and speech rather than paid employment.
[c2-l004 >] ¶ Chiyeuo fataracaſu. *Vſar, & exerci-*
[c2-l004 note] Chie wo hatarakasu is exercising intelligence or judgment. Chiye represents chie; the explicit object explains the transitive force.
[c2-l005 >] *tar o ſaber. ¶* Cuchiuo fataracaſu. *Fa-*
[c2-l005 note] After completing the intelligence example, kuchi wo hatarakasu means using the mouth, speaking. The scan's damaged rounded letter is compatible with u, and the Portuguese falar/lingua supports Cuchi rather than Cachi.
[c2-l006 >] *lar, ou exercitar a lingua.*
[c2-l006 note] Completes falar and explains exercising the tongue. This corroborates the mouth reading in the preceding Japanese example.
[c2-l007] Fataraqi, u, aita. *Trabalhar. ¶ Itẽ, Pelejar.*
[c2-l007 note] Hataraku has both working and fighting senses. The entry is intransitive here, in contrast to the preceding causative and transitive derivatives.
[c2-l008 >] ¶ Cocorono fataraita fito. *Viuo, induſ-*
[c2-l008 note] Kokoro no hataraita hito describes someone mentally alert or resourceful. The Portuguese vivo is liveliness of mind, not merely being alive.
[c2-l009 >] *trioſo, &c. ¶* Qino fataracanu fito. *Mo-*
[c2-l009 note] Completes industrioso and contrasts ki no hatarakanu hito, someone whose mind does not work actively. The negative -nu governs the interpretation.
[c2-l010 >] *langaz, ou froxo.*
[c2-l010 note] Completes molangaz and pairs it with froxo, describing sluggishness or lack of vigor. These adjectives explain the inactive-minded person, not the alert one above.
[c2-l011] Fataraqi. *Trabalho, ou ſeruiço em algũa o-*
[c2-l011 note] Hataraki is the noun, work or service in an undertaking. Its verbal origin should not lead to adding conjugational endings to this separate entry.
[c2-l012 >] *bra. ¶ Item, Batalha, ou combate.*
[c2-l012 note] Completes obra and adds battle or combat as another noun sense. This parallels the verb's military use.
[c2-l013] Fataſaxi. *O que leua abandeira diante.*
[c2-l013 note] Hatasashi is a standard-bearer carrying the flag in front. The joined abandeira is preserved, not silently respaced.
[c2-l014] Fataſaxi. *Hum certo peixe muito grande.*
[c2-l014 note] The same reading also names a very large fish. No precise modern species is inferred from this short description alone.
[c2-l015] Fatatagami. *Trouão rijo.*
[c2-l015 note] Hatatagami denotes loud or violent thunder. The native compound is kept as transmitted rather than normalized to modern kaminari.
[c2-l016] Fatato. *Adu. De improuiſo, ou derepente.*
[c2-l016 note] Hata to is an adverb meaning suddenly or unexpectedly. The Portuguese gives two synonymous expressions, retaining the printed joined derepente.
[c2-l017] Fatauori. *Hum bicho como gafanhoto.*
[c2-l017 note] Hataori names an insect resembling a grasshopper. The weaving association of the name does not establish a precise modern species from this definition alone.
[c2-l018] Fataxi, ſu, aita. *Acabar algũa couſa: ajun-*
[c2-l018 note] Hatasu means finishing something. The explanation continues with its productive attachment to other verb stems, rather than listing only one concrete action.
[c2-l019 >] *taſe este verbo às raizes de muitos, & então*
[c2-l019 note] Completes ajunta-se: this verb attaches to the roots of many others. Raizes is the dictionary's grammatical description of the bases used in these compounds.
[c2-l020 >] *quer dizer acabar o que ſignifica a raiz. Vt,*
[c2-l020 note] The compound means completing the action expressed by the base. This explains why the following reading and writing examples both end in hatasu.
[c2-l021 >] Yomi fataſu. *Acabar de ler.* Caqi
[c2-l021 note] Yomi-hatasu is finishing reading; kaki begins the next example and belongs with hatasu on the next physical row. The Portuguese confirms the first compound's meaning.
[c2-l022 >] fataſu, *Acabar de eſcreuer algũa couſa, &c.*
[c2-l022 note] Completes kaki-hatasu, finishing writing something. This is a continuation of the preceding row, not a fresh isolated headword.
[c2-l023] Fatazauo. *Haſtea da bandeira.*
[c2-l023 note] Hatazao is a flagstaff, literally the pole of a flag. Historical zao is represented by zauo; the Portuguese haste supports the object identification.
[c2-l024] Fatbǒ. Vocori qizaſu. i, Moye izzuru.
[c2-l024 note] Fatbǒ is associated with budding or sprouting: okori kizasu and moe izuru explain the emergence of growth. The compressed Sino-Japanese form is retained rather than respelled as a modern pronunciation.
[c2-l025 >] *Nacer como eruas, trigo, &c.* || {word} *( fora.*
[c2-l025 note] The main definition is sprouting, as herbs or wheat. At the far right, the printed parenthesis introduces displaced fora, which completes the following row's botar ou sacudir fora, not this growth definition.
[c2-l026] Fatbu. Bupp. Farǒ coto. *O botar, ou ſacudir*
[c2-l026 note] Harau koto explains throwing or shaking something off; fora is displaced upward to the preceding row. Bapp. is visibly Roman like the surrounding Japanese, and is retained as an abbreviated form without forcing an expansion.
[c2-l027] Fatçu, l, Fatçumono. *Primicias de qualquer*
[c2-l027 note] Hatsu or hatsumono means the first produce of something. Primicias introduces the first-fruits sense before the entry broadens to first occasions generally.
[c2-l028 >] *couſa, como fruita primeira, &c. ¶ Itẽ,*
[c2-l028 note] Completes the first-produce definition with fruit as the example. Item then marks a further use, not another kind of fruit.
[c2-l029 >] *A primeira vez que ſe faz algũa couſa, ou a*
[c2-l029 note] Hatsu can denote the first time something is done. The parallel phrase begun at the end continues with first visiting a place.
[c2-l030 >] *primeira vez que ſe vai a algum lugar, &c.*
[c2-l030 note] Completes the first-visit sense. This prepares the Miyako example, in which the speaker says this is their first arrival there.
[c2-l031 >] *Vt,* Miacoye maitta cotoua ima fatçude
[c2-l031 note] Miyako e maitta koto wa ima hatsu de continues into gozaru: this is the first time I have come to the capital. Maitta is the past form of mairu, not an unrelated lexical item.
[c2-l032 >] gozaru. *Eſta he a primeira vez q̃ vim ao*
[c2-l032 note] Gozaru completes the Japanese sentence; the Portuguese likewise says this is the first time I came to the place named on the next row.
[c2-l033 >] *Miaco. ¶* Fatçugan. *Os primeiros pa-*
[c2-l033 note] Miaco completes the preceding journey example. Hatsugan then names the first wild waterfowl taken; the Portuguese's broad bird terminology should not override the Japanese gan association with geese.
[c2-l034 >] *tos brauos q̃ ſe tomão, & aſſi de todas as ma-*
[c2-l034 note] Completes patos bravos and extends the first-taking pattern to other things. The split mais continues on the following physical row.
[c2-l035 >] *is couſas. ¶* Fatçu araxi. i, Xichi
[c2-l035 note] Hatsuarashi is the first autumn wind. The following damaged Xi. hi appears intended as Xichi, shichi, continuing into guat on the next row: the seventh lunar month. The tiny missing or worn c is not silently supplied here.
[c2-l036 >] guatno caje. *Primeiro vento que venta no*
[c2-l036 note] Completes the likely shichigatsu no kaze explanation, wind of the seventh month. Caje represents kaze, and the Portuguese identifies the first wind of the season rather than an arbitrary storm.
[c2-l037 >] *principio do Outono. ¶* Fatçufana. *As*
[c2-l037 note] The season is the beginning of autumn in the historical calendar. Hatsuhana then introduces the first flowers, an independent application of hatsu.
[c2-l038 >] *primeiras flores. ¶* Fatçu faru. i, Xo-
[c2-l038 note] After first flowers, hatsuharu is glossed by shōgatsu, split across rows. This is the first month/new year sense rather than merely early spring weather.
[c2-l039 >] guat. *Primeiro mes do anno.*
[c2-l039 note] Completes Xo-guat, shōgatsu, explicitly defined as the first month of the year. The historical final consonant notation is retained.
[c2-l040] Fatçu araxi. *Vide,* Fatçu.
[c2-l040 note] The separate hatsuarashi entry cross-refers to hatsu, where its seasonal example has just appeared. Vide is the Portuguese/Latin reference instruction, not part of the Japanese term.
[c2-l041] Fatçuburi. *Hũa laya de capacete.* || {word} *(noua.* || {usage} *P.*
[c2-l041 note] Hatsuburi is a kind of helmet. The far-right parenthesized noua and P. complete the following entry's Cousa nova; the former transcription duplicated this fragment inside the helmet definition. Physical position is retained without duplicating it.
[c2-l042] Fatçuca, l, fatçucana. i, Mezzuraxij. *Couſa*
[c2-l042 note] Hatsuka/hatsukana is equated with mezurashii, something novel or unusual. Cousa is completed by noua displaced upward, not by the next entry about birdsong.
[c2-l043] Fatçu coye. *Primeira voz, ou canto dos paſ-*
[c2-l043 note] Hatsukoe is the first voice or song of birds. Koe, not koye as two syllables, corresponds to Portuguese voz/canto.
[c2-l044 >] *ſaros no verão, ou prima vera, mas mais vſado*
[c2-l044 note] Completes passaros and places the first song in summer or spring. The sentence then qualifies which Japanese term is more usual, rather than asserting two different species.
[c2-l045 >] *he,* Fatçune.
[c2-l045 note] Hatsune is stated to be the more usual expression for the first birdsong. This Japanese term functions inside the Portuguese explanation and is visibly Roman.
[c2-l046] Fatçufana. *Vide fatçu.*
[c2-l046 note] Hatsuhana cross-refers to hatsu above. The referenced fatçu itself is italic in this row, unlike the Roman headword; the printed difference is preserved.
[c2-l047] Fatçufaru. *Vide ſupra fatçu.*
[c2-l047 note] Hatsuharu likewise refers above to hatsu. Both the instruction and the referenced word are italic here, while the entry headword remains Roman.
